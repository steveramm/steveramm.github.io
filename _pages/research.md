<div class="page-content"> <p class="research-lead">Bridging organismal and molecular perspectives on adaptive evolution and plasticity, our research programme focuses on the evolutionary ecology of reproduction.</p> </div> </div>

<div class="page-grid research-item"> <header class="page-label"> <p class="section-number">01.01</p> <h2>Climate change and fertility</h2> </header>

<div class="page-content"> <p>The ability to maintain fertility under environmental stress is a key determinant of population persistence. However, we still lack a predictive framework for how reproductive function evolves under environmental change, limiting our ability to forecast adaptive potential in natural populations. We're using both single-generation and experimental evolution approaches to better understand the capacity of organisms to adapt to chronic and acute climate change stressors - with a current emphasis on temperature and salinity impacts in _Macrostomum_ flatworms - alongside various meta-analysis projects to establish general principles of climate change adaptation.
</p> </div> </div>

<div class="page-grid research-item"> <header class="page-label"> <p class="section-number">01.02</p> <h2>Sperm competition, sexual selection and sexual conflict</h2> </header>

<div class="page-content"> <p>Post-mating sexual selection via sperm competition and cryptic female choice is a fundamental force shaping many aspects of animal reproductive morphology, physiology and behaviour, often associated with sexual conflict over reproductive interactions. Core interests of the lab in this area concern selection for and mechanisms of plasticity in sperm production traits; the evolution of animal genitalia; and the functional and evolutionary significance of seminal fluid proteins. We helped establish M. lignano as a useful model for studying all of these questions, showing for example that flatworm seminal fluid is complex, with genotype-environment interactions for seminal fluid expression that are linked to sperm competitive ability and that novel proteins in seminal fluid mediate sexual conflict of sperm fate. </p> </div> </div>

<div class="page-grid research-item"> <header class="page-label"> <p class="section-number">01.02</p> <h2>Sex allocation</h2> </header>

<div class="page-content"> <p>In hermaphroditic taxa, each individual has two potential routes to fitness and sex allocation refers to the optimal or realized division of resources between these two routes. Being transparent, Macrostomum flatworms are ideally suited for studying sex allocation decisions (we can estimate sex allocation based on the relative size of the testes and ovaries as proxies for investment into the male and female sex functions, respectively). Key questions we address include deciphering the mechanisms through which plasticity in sex allocation is achieved or on the consequences of altered conditions (such as a change in the abiotic or biotic environment, or a shift in mating system) for the optimal sex allocation.</p> </div> </div>

<div class="page-grid research-item"> <header class="page-label"> <p class="section-number">01.02</p> <h2>Selfing and mating system evolution</h2> </header>

<div class="page-content"> <p>A further consequence of hermaphroditism is that it opens up the possibility of self-fertilization. Whether individuals produce predominantly through outcrossing or selfing has profound implications for the genetics and adaptive potential of populations, and we quantify intra- and inter-specific variation in selfing propensity and how this jointly evolves with inbreeding depression and sex allocation. A current major focus of the lab is to understand the purging of deleterious alleles upon their exposure to selection after selfing, as well as on quantifying genetic and epigenetic contributions to the strength of inbreeding depression.</p> </div> </div>

**Modelling the genetic and epigenetic causes of inbreeding depression**

Mutation is a permanent, fundamental characteristic of life, and the fuel of evolution. However most mutations are deleterious, and a large corpus of population genetic studies describe the evolutionary consequences of deleterious mutations: from threatening the survival of endangered populations, to encouraging the evolution of outcrossing, sex and recombination. Central in this theory is the concept of inbreeding depression (ID), the loss of fitness in offspring of consanguineous matings. In simultaneously hermaphroditic organisms capable of self-fertilization, ID is classically measured by comparing the fitness of offspring produced by self-fertilization versus that of offspring produced by cross-fertilization, although it can also be measured using less extreme forms of inbreeding such as sib- or cousin-crosses in separate-sex organisms. ID is of major practical importance in plant breeding and animal conservation. ID is thought to emerge mainly because deleterious mutations are partially recessive, so that their effects are amplified in the homozygous condition brought about by inbreeding.

A weakness of current theory around inbreeding depression is that it ignores epigenetics, and recent experimental evidence challenges classical models by pointing to a potential role for epigenetic mechansims in generating ID over much shorter timescales than has previosuly been assumed. The internship will therefore focus on modelling these two types of variants (mutations and epimutations), both assumed to trigger deleterious effects when possessed by the two uniting gametes, in order to clarify the pace with which they can (re)generate inbreeding depression both in natural and experimental populations, and thereby assess the plausibility of different potential mechanisms underlying this fundamental evolutionary phenomenon.  

The internship will contribute to ANR project FRIDA (Fast-renewed inbreeding depression in animals), a collaboration between ECOBIO and colleagues in Montpellier, Lyon and Perpignan. Complementary experimental approaches using the free-living flatworm Macrostomum hystrix (at ECOBIO) and the freshwater snail Physa acuta (in Montpellier and Lyon) are ongoing.

**Testing for climate- and neighbourhood-driven local adaptation and adaptive plasticity in the sex allocation of oak trees**
 
For hermaphrodites, sex allocation is a crucial life history parameter, with optimal investment into male vs. female function likely impacted by the local climate and the availability of conspecific partners in the neighbourhood.
 
In order to test for adaptive variation in sex allocation in a long-lived species under natural conditions, we will use oak as a model system. Individual adult trees from the Petite Charnie common garden plantation will be sampled and sex allocation estimated using surveys of male and female flowers, profiting from existing data on growth and phenology of these trees. By comparing the sex allocation of trees from different provenances, we can test for local adaptation driven by climatic conditions and availability of conspecific partners at their provenances.
 
Moreover, we will test for realized investment into male and female functions in situ (i) along geographic gradients of climate and availability of conspecific partners using data bases from the PotenChen program, and (ii) using a long-term monitoring study in Rennes, profiting from an exceptionally well resolved dataset on urban heat island effects which vary in space and in time (more recent in more recently constructed zones) and earlier data and samples on flowering of these trees. We can compare the degree and the age of heat-island effects to the response of sex allocation in oak trees, selecting specifically old trees that were mostly there already prior to the urban development. 
 
Sex allocation will be studied during visits in March and April, visually quantifying the appearance of male and female flowers on 10 positions of both the N and the S side of the crown of a minimum of 20 trees per combination of ongoing thermal regime (cool/warm) and either past provenance (warmer/cooler) or age of construction zone (recent/old), accounting for the respective abundance of conspecifics. Thermal regimes and ages will be quantified on a continuous scale. 
 
The ultimate aim is to elucidate both abiotic and biotic factors driving sex allocation decisions over both evolutionary and ecological timescales.

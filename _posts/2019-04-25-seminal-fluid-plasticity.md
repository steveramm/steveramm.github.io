Hermaphrodites represent interesting model systems in which to study seminal fluid-mediated fitness effects, both to test whether we can generalise from insights gained so far in separate-sexed organisms, and also because having a mating partner who is a hermaphrodite presents some unique avenues to potentially manipulate their reproductive physiology and behaviour. Specifically, seminal fluid can potentially target now not only the female but also the male sex function of that partner. One species in which such an effect has already been demonstrated is the great pond snail, _Lymnaea stagnalis_. During her Alexander von Humboldt postdoc fellowship in the lab, [Yumi Nakadera](https://www.uni-bielefeld.de/\(en\)/biologie/Evolutionsbiologie/mitarbeiter/nakadera.html) has been building on these insights to address a related prediction that also appears to occur in separate-sexed organisms (e.g. [mice](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12915-015-0197-2), [flies](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1558-5646.2010.01141.x) and [crickets](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2017.0659)), namely the idea that individuals should strategically invest in seminal fluid production according to their social environment. [We found](https://doi.org/10.1093/beheco/arz027) that there is indeed a big up-regulation of seminal fluid production in the presence compared to the absence of a mating partner, including for proteins with demonstrated effects on [male](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2014.02.052) and [female](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0010117) allocation of partners, but saw no evidence for any additional alterations to seminal fluid production as the number of snails in a group (and thus the amount of competition over fertilisation) increased. Patterns of plasticity were highly consistent for most of the different seminal fluid genes we measured, which fits well with the coordinated pattern of seminal fluid co-expression [we found recently](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41437-018-0160-4) also in flatworms.

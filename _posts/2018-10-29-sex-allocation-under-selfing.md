![Macrostomum\_hystrix\_worm]({{ '/assets/images/sex-allocation-under-selfing/macrostomum_hystrix_worm.jpg' | relative_url }})The number of sperm produced by males usually far exceeds the number of eggs produced by females. The same principle also applies in hermaphrodites, where each individual typically makes many more sperm than eggs. However, if they cannot find a mate, some hermaphrodites have evolved an alternative route by which to reproduce: using their own sperm to fertilise their eggs. Such ‘selfing’ behaviour relaxes the pressure to produce so much sperm, and in a recent study published this month in [Biology Letters](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2018.0570) we demonstrate that flatworms indeed flexibly adjust sperm production to match with whether they are forced to self or can mate with other individuals. This result extends the [observation](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2002.2323) of phenotypic plasticity in sex allocation in _Macrostomum_ flatworms to a mixed-mating species. In the parlance of the [NC3 project](https://www.uni-bielefeld.de/biologie/crc212/) currently underway in Bielefeld, Münster and Jena, we would say that sex allocation plasticity is thus likely to be an additive response that enables individuals to tailor their sperm production and thereby conform to their prevailing social niche.

The experiment was performed by [Lennart Winkler](https://twitter.com/lennarthamburg) as his Bachelor thesis project  - congratulations Lennart!
